<h2>The eight stage process &mdash; your step by step guide</h2>

<p class="intro">
  Consider this page to be your user guide to adopting Beaverdam. The tabs along the top walk the stages of the process &mdash; what you&rsquo;ll do, what your AI guide does for you, and where
  to get the details &mdash; and, just as important, <strong>how the pieces fit together</strong> into one system that keeps working for you over the long haul.
</p>

<p class="intro">
  This page itemizes the whole process first, so nothing further up is a surprise.  This analogy might help explain why we&rsquo;re doing what we&rsquo;re doing; why you&rsquo;re not just getting a big fat "Download Beaverdam Installer" button.
</p>

<blockquote class="chain-narrative">
  To get a heavy chain over a high beam you don&rsquo;t throw the chain. You throw a
  <strong>shoe on a string</strong>; the string pulls a <strong>rope</strong>; the rope pulls
  the <strong>chain</strong>. Beaverdam climbs the same way &mdash; a series of light steps,
  each one easy enough to succeed, each one pulling the next into reach.
</blockquote>

<h3>Two things to keep straight about the time</h3>

<ul class="pairing-list">
  <li>
    <strong>One-time setup vs. ongoing-at-your-pace.</strong> Steps 1&ndash;5 are mostly a

    you choose, with no clock running.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>Beaverdam&rsquo;s &ldquo;overhead&rdquo; vs. your ERP&rsquo;s own time.</strong>
    Installing the tools and learning the system is overhead Beaverdam adds. But the biggest
    block &mdash; Step 8, putting your real data and decisions into the system &mdash; is time
    you&rsquo;d spend with <em>any</em> ERP. Beaverdam guides and shortens it; it doesn&rsquo;t
    invent it.
  </li>

<h3>Getting Claude Code onto your machine</h3>

<p>

  text command window). Unlike the chat on this website &mdash; which lives in the cloud and
  can only talk &mdash; Claude Code runs <em>on your machine</em>, so it can read and change
  files there, with your say-so. Installing it is a short, guided process whatever
  you&rsquo;re on &mdash; Windows, Mac, or Linux &mdash; with Nick walking you through the
  exact steps for your system. Nothing here you have to puzzle out alone.
</p>

<h3>Two AIs, working together &mdash; even across two machines</h3>

<p>

<h3>First, &ldquo;git&rdquo; &mdash; the tool, not the company</h3>

<p>
  <strong>git</strong> (lower-case) is a tool for coordinating changes to a body of work with <em>tens of thousands</em> of people tinkering with it, independently, 
  all at the same time. It keeps a perfect history, lets everyone work without treading on each other&rsquo;s
  toes, and merges in the good changes together cleanly. Beaverdam leans on git for three things:
  a single <strong>source of truth</strong>, a long <strong>institutional memory</strong>, and
  <strong>reviewed change</strong> &mdash; nothing slips in unseen.
</p>

<h3>The ways Beaverdam uses git</h3>

<div class="pains pains-gain">

  <div class="pain">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">Source of truth</span>
    <h3>Every line, versioned.</h3>
    <p>
      All of Beaverdam&rsquo;s code <em>and</em> your own customisations live here, with a
      full history. Nothing is abandoned on someone&rsquo;s laptop somewhere, and any change can be traced &mdash; or rolled back &mdash; to the exact moment it was made.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="pain">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">Institutional memory</span>
    <h3>Issues remember the &ldquo;why&rdquo;.</h3>
    <p>
      Every bug, task, and decision becomes a numbered, searchable <strong>issue</strong>.
      Years later you can read <em>why</em> something was done a certain way. The reasoning
      survives people coming and going &mdash; the exact problem Beaverdam is built to solve.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="pain">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">Reviewed change</span>
    <h3>Nothing slips in unseen.</h3>
    <p>
      Changes arrive as <strong>pull requests</strong> &mdash; written, reviewable
      proposals. Nothing reaches your live system without a record, and an AI reviewer
      checks each one for mistakes before it&rsquo;s allowed to land.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="pain">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">Automation</span>
    <h3>The chores run themselves.</h3>
    <p>
      <strong>Actions</strong> are robots that wake up on every change: they run the tests
      automatically, and they rebuild and publish things for you &mdash; including this very
      website, which redeploys itself whenever its words change.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="pain">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">This website</span>
    <h3>Pages, served for free.</h3>
    <p>
      The page you&rsquo;re reading is served straight from a GitHub repository by a feature
      called <strong>Pages</strong> &mdash; no separate web host, no monthly bill for it.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="pain">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">Your private vault</span>
    <h3>Not everything is public.</h3>
    <p>
      Tenant-specific knowledge and the AI&rsquo;s working memory live in <strong>private
      repositories</strong> under the same roof &mdash; same durability and history, visible
      only to you.
    </p>
  </div>

</div>

<h3>Where git lives &mdash; GitHub, and the alternatives</h3>

<p>
  While <b>git</b> is the tool; you are still going to need a place online to keep and share the repositories with your staff. Several services do
  that job: <strong>GitHub</strong>, <strong>GitLab</strong>, <strong>Gitea</strong> (which
  you can even host yourself) and many others. Beaverdam uses GitHub by default &mdash; and it&rsquo;s no
  fly-by-night startup: it hosts most of the world&rsquo;s largest free and open-source
  projects, and since 2018 it has been a division of Microsoft. About as durable as software
  homes get. GitLab and Gitea are open alternatives if you&rsquo;d rather; it&rsquo;s a real
  choice, and Nick can talk you through it.
</p>

<h3>Signing up, and your SSH keys</h3>

<p>
  You create a <strong>free</strong> account (a couple of minutes). Then, so your machine can have a secure, private channel into GitHub, you generate an <strong>SSH key</strong> &mdash; <em>SSH</em> is
  &ldquo;Secure Shell,&rdquo; and the key is a matched pair: a <em>public</em> half you hand to
  GitHub, and a <em>private</em> half that never leaves your control.
</p>

<p>
  Guard that private half like a house key. We&rsquo;ll want you to keep a copy somewhere safe
  &mdash; on a <strong>thumb drive</strong> tucked away, or even <strong>printed as a QR code
  and kept in a firesafe</strong>. Lose it and you simply make a new one, but it&rsquo;s yours
  to protect. Nick handles the technical side of setting it up on your system.
</p>

<h3>Bringing Beaverdam home &mdash; and meeting Nick</h3>

<p>
  With the account and key in place, you <strong>clone</strong> the Beaverdam project &mdash;
  git&rsquo;s word for downloading your own working copy. Then comes a small but pivotal move:
  you stop Claude Code where it&rsquo;s running now, and restart it <em>inside</em> the
  Beaverdam project folder. From that moment Claude Code is loaded with everything Beaverdam
  &mdash; and that is when it truly becomes <strong>Nick</strong>, the keeper of your own copy,
  rather than general-purpose Claude.
</p>

<h3>Why this is in your long-term interest</h3>

<p>
  Because the system&rsquo;s code, history, and reasoning all live in one open, portable
  place, the business is never trapped. If the person helping you changes &mdash; a year from
  now, or ten &mdash; the next one opens the same public history and picks up exactly where
  things stood, with nothing locked in someone&rsquo;s memory. That is ownership and
  continuity made concrete: the precise opposite of the single-developer trap.
</p>

<div class="split">

<h3>Harmonizing! (Important)</h3>

<p>
To do great work for you your staff needs to understand and gain inspiration from your vision and your mission.  With AI workers it is actually much <strong>more</strong> important. AIs will happily work towards what they imagine you goal is.  If they get it wrong, it will all go wrong, and you will have a hard time figuring out why.
</p>

<p>
Nick has instructions to talk you through defining your specific Mission and Vision; basically, "what you are setting out do" and "what the world will look like when you have done it".  Those are the boundary lines and goal posts your AI helpers will work within and towards.
</p>

<h3>Where your control centre lives</h3>

<p>

  <em>where</em> it lives, and that&rsquo;s a serious conversation. You and Nick weigh up the machines you have, what you&rsquo;re comfortable running, and what it needs. Nick has done this many times and will patiently walk you through the options to find the most suitable site.
</p>

<p>
  Once you&rsquo;ve picked a home, Nick helps you put the plumbing in place &mdash; each piece
  explained as you go:
</p>

<ul class="pairing-list">
  <li>

    <code>your-business.com</code>), which you buy and own.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>A Cloudflare account</strong> &mdash; the service that points your domain at the
    right machine and shields it from hostile traffic.
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>A wildcard SSL certificate</strong> &mdash; one padlock that secures your address
    <em>and every sub-address under it</em> (so <code>shop.your-business.com</code>,
    <code>books.your-business.com</code> and the rest are all encrypted at once).
  </li>
  <li>
    <strong>WireGuard &amp; DNS</strong> &mdash; WireGuard is a <strong>Virtual Private
    Network (VPN)</strong>, private encrypted pathways between your machines; DNS (the
    <strong>Domain Name System</strong>) is the address book that matches names to the right
    machine.
  </li>
</ul>

<p>
  This is <strong>Paco&rsquo;s</strong> territory. Your machines can move around &mdash; a
  virtual machine on a laptop today, a rented server abroad tomorrow &mdash; and Paco keeps
  every name pointing at the right place even after things have shifted, so <em>you</em> never
  chase a broken address.
</p>

<h3>What saconsole runs</h3>

<p>
  Two things live on saconsole. One is the <a href="#step7">live map</a> (coming up in Step 7).
  The other is <strong>Grafana</strong> &mdash; your monitoring: it turns the raw numbers your
  machines produce into readable dashboards, so the health of everything is one glance away,
  and it warns you <em>before</em> a small problem becomes an outage.
</p>

       loading="lazy">

</p>

<h2>Step 7 &mdash; The live map: seeing all your machines</h2>

<p class="intro">

<h3>The four zones of the map</h3>

<div class="quadrants">

  <div class="quadrant q-saconsole">
    <span class="tag">Top-left &mdash; your base</span>
    <h3>saconsole &amp; the templates.</h3>
    <p>
      Your control centre from Step 6, plus a shelf of <strong>ready-made templates</strong>
      &mdash; pre-built quick-start systems to solve problems fast and to save your time and AI credits from being wasted redoing from-scratch builds over and over again.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="quadrant q-dev">
    <span class="tag gain-tag">Top-right &mdash; green</span>

    <p>
      <strong>Virtual machines (VMs)</strong> &mdash; whole computers simulated in software

      <strong>Apache CloudStack</strong> and providers such as
      <a href="https://www.iwstack.com/">iwStack</a> come in.
    </p>
  </div>

  <div class="quadrant q-prod">
    <span class="tag pain-tag">Bottom-right &mdash; red</span>
    <h3>Production.</h3>
    <p>
      The real, live system &mdash; on <strong>Virtual Private Servers (VPSs)</strong>:
      your own space in a powerful machine in a professional data centre, in any of many
      places around the world.
    </p>
  </div>

</div>

<h3>Why production lives in more than one place</h3>

<p>
  For the live system, Beaverdam can spread you across <strong>several providers and
  countries</strong> &mdash; for example an account with <a href="https://contabo.com/en/vps/">Contabo</a>
  in the USA and another with <a href="https://www.prometeus.net/">Prometeus</a> in Europe.
  A main copy (the <strong>master</strong>) is continuously replicating all data to a standby copy (the <strong>slave</strong>), so if one fails the other carries on or takes over completely.
</p>

<p>
  The point of using two far-apart homes is that they share <em>nothing</em> that could fail
  at once: separate electricity, separate geography (a tsunami, tornado, or volcano that hits
  one can&rsquo;t hit both), and separate undersea data cables (which matters in an age of
  deliberate cable-cutting). It&rsquo;s the digital version of not keeping all your records in
  one building.
</p>

<p class="comment">
  Those are example hosting services, not endorsements &mdash; Nick helps you choose, weighing price and fit:
  <a href="https://www.iwstack.com/">iwStack</a> &middot;
  <a href="https://contabo.com/en/vps/">Contabo</a> &middot;
  <a href="https://www.prometeus.net/">Prometeus</a> &middot;
  <a href="https://cloudstack.apache.org/">Apache CloudStack</a> (the software behind
  pay-by-the-minute clouds).
</p>
